The missing layer

Your resources already exist. The connection doesn’t.

Doctors, counsellors, hospitals, faculty, student services and technology. Each holds a part of the picture.

A struggling student meets none of it as a system. They meet a list of places they have never been, none of which knows they are coming. So they go nowhere, and the first anyone hears of it is when something has already broken.

In plain terms

We organise around the person, not the other way round.

A room on your campus with people in it. Students, faculty and families can all walk in. You do not need an appointment or a referral letter, and there is no waiting list.

If someone needs more than a conversation, we find them the right professional and go with them until they have arrived. Nobody is given a phone number and left to it.

A Lythous node is not a clinic or a counselling service. Nobody who walks in becomes a patient. When professional care is needed we refer to an independent qualified professional, and from that point the relationship is with them.

How it works

Seven steps, and somebody owns every one.

01

Recognition

Trained Ambassadors notice what a timetable misses. The student always alone, the one who stopped arriving.

02

Engagement

A conversation on the person’s terms. In the room, or by phone, text or WhatsApp.

03

Navigation

We work out what would help, and which of the university’s existing resources fits.

04

Professional care

We refer to an independent qualified professional where that is needed. From then on the relationship is with them.

05

Escalation

A written protocol covers anyone at risk. It names who is contacted, in what order, and how quickly.

06

Referral

We stay with the person until they have actually arrived, and we check that they did.

07

Follow-through

Someone checks back afterwards, and records that they did.

Why now

Indian institutions are being asked to do more than appoint a counsellor.

Recent Supreme Court and UGC guidance sets out what higher-education institutions are expected to have in place: a mental health policy, qualified support or formal referral linkages, written escalation protocols, staff training, parent sensitisation and anonymised annual reporting.

Most universities already hold pieces of that. What is usually missing is the layer that makes them work as one system around a student.

Who we serve

Universities and colleges

“How do we support thousands of students, not only the ones who walk into counselling?”

The reality

Most institutions already have counsellors, doctors, wardens and student services. A student who is struggling meets none of it as a system. They meet a list of places they have never been, none of which knows they are coming.

What a node adds

  • A staffed room anyone can walk into, with no appointment and no referral letter
  • Trained Ambassadors whose job is to notice the student who never asks
  • A written escalation protocol naming who is contacted, in what order and how quickly
  • A formal referral linkage to independent qualified professionals
  • Somebody who stays with the student until they have arrived, and checks back afterwards

What leadership sees

Anonymised utilisation, engagement and follow-through, and an annual report. Never individual names.

Hospitals and healthcare

“What happens to a person between appointments?”

The reality

The gap is rarely clinical capacity. It is the weeks between encounters, when nobody is responsible for the person and nobody finds out that they stopped coming.

What a node adds

  • A non-clinical layer around the clinical one
  • Recognition, navigation and follow-through between appointments
  • A person who confirms the handoff actually happened

A node delivers no care and sits outside your clinical governance. Referral goes to independent qualified professionals, and from that point the relationship is with them.

For professionals

Where you fit

“Where do I fit, and does my practice stay mine?”

You practise in your own right. A Lythous™ node refers people to you. It does not employ you to deliver care, and it does not deliver care itself.

What that means in practice

  • From the point of referral, the relationship is between you and the person
  • Your credentials, your standards and your record keeping remain yours
  • Referrals arrive with context, not as a name and a number
